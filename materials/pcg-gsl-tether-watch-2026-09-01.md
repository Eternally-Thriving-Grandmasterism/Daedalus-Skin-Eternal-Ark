## 2. What ISEC actually requires (Earth elevator, not Daedalus)

An Earth elevator is a tapered tether from an equatorial marine anchor through GEO (~35,786 km) to an apex counterweight. Working length is on the order of **100,000 km** (press often writes 66,000 miles).

ISEC working requirement, as stated in their tether-materials stream:

- Tensile strength **above ~80 GPa** at density **below ~3 g/cm³** so the ribbon can hold itself (~1,000 t) plus a ~20 t climber.
- Historical design targets sit near **100 GPa** or ~38–100 MYuri specific strength.
- Climber baseline in ISEC papers: ~20 t vehicle, ~14 t payload, ~4 MW, roughly one climb per day, ~7 days to GEO then another week to the apex.
- First-class (not afterthought) requirements: interlayer shear, friction coefficient **>0.1**, conductivity, heat rejection.
- ISEC climber-tether study: van der Waals bonding between graphene layers is about **35× too weak** to carry a 20 t climber without slip unless layers are cross-linked.

A finished ISEC tether concept is **Graphene Super Laminate (GSL)** — stacked 2D sheets, described as ~12,000 atoms thick at the fat point — **not** a single monolayer and **not** raw electronics-grade film.

## 3. Material ladder (conditional numbers)

| Class | Cited strength | Manufacturing state (2026) | PATSAGi read |
|-------|----------------|----------------------------|--------------|
| Single-crystal graphene | ~130 GPa theory | Lab sheets at half-metre class, not industrial kilometres | Theoretical ceiling, not a product |
| Polycrystalline CVD graphene (PCG) | ISEC cites **90–99 GPa if** grain boundaries are well-stitched and vacancy density is low | 1 km roll-to-roll exists | The “if” is the whole argument. Treat as conditional lab/model result, not a certified spec |
| Carbon nanotubes | 77–200 GPa ideal tubes | Useful continuous fibre still few-GPa class at engineering lengths | Backup. Manufacturing trajectory currently loses to graphene sheet production |
| Hexagonal boron nitride | ~100 GPa theory | Manufacturing lag vs graphene | Backup / possible co-laminate |

Grain boundaries are 5–8 member rings joining misoriented crystals. They nucleate cracks. A 100,000 km cable is a weakest-link problem (Pugno megamechanics still applies). Strength on a micron or centimetre coupon is not the strength of a planet-to-GEO ribbon.

## 4. What South Korea actually makes

CharmGraphene public specs (ISEC slides and company process claims):

- Roll-to-roll CVD graphene
- ~**2 m/min**
- Lengths up to **1 km**
- Typical width **300 mm** (later equipment claims 600 mm)
- Product: electronics-grade film on thin copper foil (pellicles, heaters, shielding)

Graphene Square is the other large-area Korean CVD player; same category. LG is cited by ISEC at ~1 m/min, ~1 km, ~400 mm foil.

ISEC slides say this explicitly: impressive industrial PCG, **“not yet tether quality,”** “polycrystalline layered graphene rather than a van der Waals homostructure.” Multilayer samples up to ~30 atoms thick have been shown. That is not 12,000-layer shear-locked GSL.

**Separation the lattice will not blur:**

- Sheet exists at 1 km ≠ laminate exists at tether cross-section.
- Electronics film ≠ cross-linked climber-grade ribbon.

## 6. Tonnage and cost (press distortion)

- One elevator at IOC ≈ 14 t payload/day ≈ **~5,110 t/yr** (ISEC logistics papers).
- **“30,000 t/yr” is a fleet number** (about six elevators / three “Galactic Harbours”), not day-one output of one tether.
- Full operational capability is later quoted near **170,000 t/yr**.
- Swan advocacy figure: **~$15B** for a first robotic system. Independent commentary treats this as advocacy, not a bottoms-up bill of materials.
- Obayashi public industrial target remains ~2050 and has already slipped earlier start dates.

## 7. Unsolved stack, ranked by physics not PR

1. Tether-quality material at length: well-stitched grains, controlled vacancies, proven 80–100 GPa at metre-plus coupons, then kilometre coupons.
2. Interlayer shear / climber grip: spot-weld or other covalent cross-link so layers do not slide under a 20 t wheel.
3. Debris: LEO is denser than Edwards-era (2003) studies. ISEC answer is multiple tethers plus a no-fly zone — policy plus armor, not a solved impact model.
4. Atomic oxygen: ISEC estimates graphite-like erosion of tens of nm/year in LEO; GSL is argued to beat gold coatings, still a repair-budget item.
5. Deployment: unspooling ~100,000 km from GEO without entanglement, oscillation, or snap-back.
6. Weather, lightning, dynamo currents, thermal cycling, micrometeoroids above LEO.
7. Funding and liability: multi-decade cash-flow; insurance for a structure that can cut orbital traffic.

Consensus outside ISEC advocacy: physically allowed, **not engineering-ready in 2026**. “Closer than ever” is a fair materials-trajectory sentence. “Ready to build” is not.
